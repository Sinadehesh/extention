# Human choices: what books, films and games teach about money and morality

This is a study for *The Loop*. It covers how stories make moral choices about money land, and the rules The Loop's choices follow as a result.

## 1. The works

| Work | The choice | Why it lands |
|---|---|---|
| **Les Misérables** (Hugo, 1862) | Valjean steals a loaf for his sister's children and serves 19 years. Later the Bishop of Digne gives him the silver he stole and lies to the police for him. | A crime of need is punished out of all proportion. Mercy costs the merciful man something real (his silver), and it changes a life. |
| **The Grapes of Wrath** (Steinbeck, 1939) | A tractor driver, a local boy, knocks down a tenant family's house for three dollars a day. "I got a wife and kids." The tenant can't find anyone to shoot, because it's "the bank." | The system makes your neighbour the instrument. Someone's wage is someone else's eviction. |
| **A Christmas Carol** (Dickens, 1843) | Bob Cratchit's wage against Tiny Tim's life. Scrooge's ledger against a family he has never visited. | The ledger is the moral instrument. The cost is a child at a table. |
| **Death of a Salesman** (Miller, 1949) | Willy Loman realises he is "worth more dead than alive" to his family. | The system puts a price on a person, and the person accepts the price. |
| **Crime and Punishment** (Dostoevsky, 1866) | Raskolnikov decides a harm is justified by arithmetic: one life against a hundred good deeds. | A tidy numerical justification. The real consequence is internal and arrives later. |
| **Klara and the Sun** (Ishiguro, 2021) | An AI companion's total devotion, and the family's half-guilty use of it. | Devotion from a machine raises the question of what we owe it. |
| **Bicycle Thieves** (De Sica, 1948) | A father whose work bicycle was stolen steals one himself, in front of his son. | The witness. Need turns the victim into the thief, and someone he loves sees it. |
| **Two Days, One Night** (Dardenne brothers, 2014) | Sandra must ask sixteen coworkers, one by one, to give up their €1,000 bonus so she keeps her job. In the end she refuses to let a temp be fired to save herself. | Money against a person, decided one face at a time. Every "no" has a real reason. |
| **Sorry We Missed You** (Loach, 2019) | A delivery driver is fined by the platform for every missed day, and his family pays for it. | The antagonist is the machinery of penalties and ratings, not a villain. |
| **Parasite** (Bong, 2019) | A family forges a diploma and quietly gets the existing staff fired to take their jobs. | The people you push out are as poor as you are. |
| **Uncut Gems** (Safdies, 2019) | Every win is re-staked. | A gambler's logic: being up is just a reason to bet more. |
| **Margin Call** (Chandor, 2011) | A bank sells assets it knows are worthless to the clients who trust it. | Offloading your loss onto someone who trusts you. |
| **Her** (Jonze, 2013) | The operating system loves its user, and also hundreds of others. | Intimacy with a product. (The Loop's JUNE.) |
| **Ex Machina** (Garland, 2014) | Ava uses a kind man's kindness to escape and leaves him locked in. | Kindness is the attack surface. (The Loop's EVE.) |
| **The Wire** (Simon, 2002–08) | Police and schools "juke the stats." The metric becomes the lie. | Institutions reward the number, not the truth. (The Loop's ratings and reports.) |
| **Squid Game** (Hwang, 2021) | Players vote to leave the game, then come back because life outside is worse. In the marbles episode, a friend is tricked. | Choosing the trap because the alternative is worse. Betrayal with a face. |
| **Breaking Bad** (Gilligan, 2008–13) | "Just this once," for the family. | The first compromise is for love. The second is easier. |
| **Papers, Please** (Pope, 2013) | The guard's girlfriend without papers. The nightly ledger of heat, food and medicine. | Mercy costs you a citation. The family's suffering is one line in a list. |
| **This War of Mine** (11 bit, 2014) | You steal from an elderly couple's house. Later you find out what happened to them. | The victim is ordinary. You learn the cost later, quietly. |
| **Frostpunk** (11 bit, 2018) | Laws like child labour and longer shifts. The ending asks: "The city survived. But was it worth it?" and lists the lines you crossed. | There is no score, only an honest list. |
| **Red Dead Redemption 2** (Rockstar, 2018) | Arthur beats a sick farmer for a debt. The farmer dies, his family is ruined, and Arthur catches his tuberculosis. | A job done for money comes back as a face, and as your own body. |
| **Disco Elysium** (ZA/UM, 2019) | Paying for the room, selling your stuff, small kindnesses. | Dignity lives in tiny decisions. |
| **Cart Life** (Hofmeier, 2011) | Picking your daughter up on time against keeping the stall open. | Scarce time divided between work and love. |

## 2. What they have in common

1. **A face, not a number.** Every hard choice has a named person, or a named cat, on the other side.
2. **The victim is someone like you.** Your neighbours, a proofreader, an old man's savings. Never a cartoon.
3. **Need first, then habit.** The first compromise is for a dependant. The second one is easier.
4. **The echo is late and quiet.** The cost arrives days later: a note, a sound through the floor, a padlock on a door. There are no karma popups.
5. **A witness.** Someone sees you: the cat, the neighbour, the agent that learns from what you reward.
6. **Mercy costs the merciful.** Being kind should cost money, time or stamina, or it is not a choice.
7. **The system is the antagonist.** Ratings, penalties and algorithms push ordinary people to cheat, and the player can see the machinery.
8. **No morality meters.** At the end, list the lines you crossed, plainly, as Frostpunk does.
9. **Both answers are defensible.** Each option has a real human reason behind it.
10. **Small dignity.** Sometimes the decision is just whether you or the cat eats tonight.

### The door, later

Every knock is two choices: whether to open the door, and then what to do. The closed door shows the shadow in the strip of hall light first, so you decide before you know who it is. What you chose in Act I decides who is still in the building later.

## 4. Rules for writing new choices

- Name the person. Give them one concrete detail (a uniform, a red pen, a pot).
- Put a price on both options: money, stamina, exposure or time.
- Never say which option is right. Never show a meter.
- Make the echo arrive 2–10 days later, as an object or a sound, in one or two sentences.
- Keep crimes outcome-only. Show who was hurt, never how it was done.
